Will AI replace workers? The cost of automation

A voice bot was supposed to take pressure off Commonwealth Bank’s support desk. The bank announced 45 job cuts. But workers told the union calls were rising. Staff were offered overtime and even team leaders had to take calls. A month later the bank reversed the cuts. How can a company test the promised savings while the people who know the work are still there?

Anton Konnov · 27 November 2026

In brief
  • 21% of support leaders in a Gartner survey reported cutting staff because of AI.
  • 7% of all respondents had hired again after cuts.
  • In the worked example below, the cost advantage disappears when 32% of cases need extra manual work.

The bank reversed its cuts. Meta called off only the second round.

Twenty-five years at the bank. Then her job was on the list.

Sullivan helped write the scripts staff used to respond to customers over messaging. In July 2025, she and 44 colleagues were told their jobs would be cut. “I wasn’t even thinking about AI until my job got replaced by a Chatbot,” she told the union.

The bank reversed its decision in August. According to the Finance Sector Union, affected staff were offered a choice: stay in their roles or leave with a payment. The bank could reverse the decision. It could not take back the conversation in which Sullivan was told her job was going.

Meta changed more code. The work did not go away.

According to Reuters’ investigation, Project OT considered shrinking some teams by up to 60%. This was the upper end of the scenarios for certain units, not a plan to lay off 60% of Meta. On May 20, 2026, the company cut about 10% of its workforce. It called off preparations for the November round before the first round went ahead. Meta said it had been considering reassignments as well as layoffs and had never expected to proceed with every scenario.

Internal material seen by Reuters showed changes to internal tools and infrastructure rising 220% year-on-year, while changes delivering new or improved features to users rose 36%. Major technical and security incidents rose 40%, and staff time spent dealing with them rose 70%. Reuters did not establish which incidents AI caused or exactly why management called off the second round.

You can get code written sooner and spend longer fixing problems after release. For a team, what matters is when the feature reaches customers and how much time it takes to keep it working.

The bot replied. Did it help?

For “Where is my order?”, looking up the order is usually enough. If the customer was charged twice, someone has to check the payments and decide what to refund. AI can pull up the records. A member of staff takes responsibility for the refund.

If the shift now spends six hours on enquiries instead of eight, it has two hours back. The shift still has to be paid. The business saves money if it can get by without overtime or an extra hire. If the team uses those two hours to clear difficult cases, the benefit is a shorter wait for customers.

If the customer comes back with the same question, the bot has not closed the ticket. It has added another one. Count resolved cases and repeat contacts, rather than celebrating a faster first reply on its own.

Where AI helped, and where it added work

Gartner asked 275 service and support leaders in July and August 2026. Twenty-one per cent said they had already cut staff because of AI. Where cuts took place, the average reduction was 16.7% over the previous 18 months. But 7% of all those surveyed had hired again after earlier reductions, with another 11% planning to do so within 18 months. Hiring freezes (45%) and moves into other departments (39%) could overlap.

The same survey asked about money. Only 4% of customer service organisations said they had achieved the return they expected. The answers do not tell us how much the organisations that cut staff saved, or whether the others lost money.

“Three in a hundred” does not mean the other 97 saved nothing. In Gartner’s October release, 3% is the share that exceeded cost savings targets through productivity gains. A company that planned to save a million and saved two hundred thousand missed its target. It still saved money. Gartner also reported that 38% of companies had exceeded their AI budget and 85% intended to spend more the following year. These are survey findings, not audited company accounts.

When an assistant really saves time

There are measured gains, too. A study of 5,172 support agents found that access to an assistant raised resolved issues per hour by an average of 15%, with larger gains for less experienced workers. That was help, not replacement. In a different setting, METR’s experiment found that experienced developers using early-2025 AI tools took 19% longer on tasks in familiar repositories. This is not a measurement of today’s models: METR’s follow-up encountered selection problems with participants and tasks.

In the working paper Firm Data on AI, over 80% of surveyed firms reported no impact on employment or productivity over the preceding three years. The survey covered nearly 6,000 executives in the US, UK, Germany and Australia. A support desk can get 15% faster without making a visible difference in the company’s overall results. The two studies measured different things.

In Denmark, researchers paired worker surveys with administrative records. The latest survey round covered 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces. Chatbot users reported saving 2.8% of their working time on average. But after two years the authors found no detectable change in earnings or recorded hours and ruled out average effects larger than 2% in that sample. Time saved at a desk does not always show up on a payslip.

The jobs that never get advertised

A department can keep one analyst instead of hiring a second without laying anyone off. It may be harder for a trainee to get a first assignment while the team still needs an experienced person to stand behind the work. If a cheaper service draws in new customers, the same team might end up doing more business and hiring more people.

Using US ADP payroll data through June 2026, Stanford Digital Economy Lab researchers found that employment among 22-to-25-year-olds in more exposed occupations was 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less exposed peers. The gap came mainly through reduced hiring rather than rising separations. It is a pattern, not proof that AI caused the entire gap. A separate study across 41 countries found that foreign affiliates of AI-adopting firms employed a smaller share of junior workers mainly because senior employment grew. A smaller share is not the same as a smaller number.

Stanford’s November indicators show a year-on-year comparison as well. In September 2026, employment in the ADP sample was 2.2% lower for people aged 22–34 but 0.4% higher for those aged 35 and over. Among 22-to-25-year-olds in the two job groups with the highest AI exposure, employment was down nearly 4%. These numbers describe firms in the ADP sample, not the entire US labour market.

Challenger counted 121,401 announced US job cuts from January to October 2026 for which employers cited AI. That is neither a full record of the labour market nor proof that a model now does the work of everyone affected. And the ILO’s global index finds some potential exposure in occupations held by one in four workers worldwide. Only 3.3% of employment is in the highest category, with clerical jobs most exposed. A task the technology could change is not a job that has already been lost.

What Russian employers reported. HSE’s institute for statistical studies, using Rosstat data, found that 5.2% of surveyed large and midsize organisations used AI in 2025. Among adopters, 62.7% reported better business processes, 24.1% higher labour productivity and 9.1% lower headcount. The survey includes AI beyond generative models and excludes small businesses. Adoption, faster work and fewer jobs are not interchangeable measures.

Beginners learn on ordinary jobs. Someone reconciling invoices for the first time may discover that a supplier entered the same delivery twice under different numbers. Give every one of those jobs to the software and who will spot the error in its report in three years? Leave people work they can do on their own and then review with an experienced colleague.

Where the AI bill goes

The subscription is easy to see. The time people spend updating the knowledge base, checking replies and stepping in when the system fails is not on the provider’s bill. Connections to the business’s systems and retries cost money too. Cut staff too soon and you can add severance, rehiring and training. Calculate what it costs to resolve a case, not what a token costs.

Who else gets the bill?

At the supplier, revenue comes in before the customer knows what return they will earn. NVIDIA reported $115.8 billion of revenue and $65.3 billion of net income for the quarter ended October 25, 2026. This shows the scale of one supplier’s sales and earnings, not the return on its customers’ AI deployments. The IMF points to circular financing: companies can be each other’s suppliers, customers and financiers. That alone does not make a transaction fictitious. The risk is that independent demand will fall short while the commitments and interest payments remain.

On the power grid, the question is who gets the bill. The independent market monitor for PJM put the cumulative increase in capacity market costs associated with existing and projected data centre demand at $29.4 billion in that market. It is not a bill sent to households or a forecast of tariffs in another country. Local rules decide how costs are allocated.

Buy a server and you pay for it even while it sits idle. Use the cloud and there is no server to buy, but a busy week might cost more than you expect. Check what a spike costs and how hard it would be to switch providers. If an unusual request sends the AI back to the start, the retries belong on the bill too.

The maths: 2,000 support requests a month

Take a small support desk with 2,000 enquiries a month and the same service standard in both setups. An enquiry takes twelve minutes by hand, and one working hour costs the company RUB 1,500. With AI, an agent spends four minutes on every reply, plus another fifteen minutes on 15% of tickets. Model usage, operations and support cost RUB 160,000 a month. These are example figures, not company results.

Value the time at the hourly rate and subtract the technology bill, and the difference is RUB 127,500 a month. A one-off setup and integration cost of RUB 450,000 would pay for itself in about 3.5 months if the company could realise that saving each month. If wages stay the same, you still pay RUB 600,000 for the team, plus RUB 160,000 for the technology. In return, you get about 192 hours back. Decide what to do with them before treating those hours as a saving.

Reviews and technology cost RUB 360,000 a month before you deal with any difficult tickets. Each one adds RUB 375. At 640 of them, or 32% of requests, AI costs the same as doing the work by hand. At 35% the cost is RUB 622,500. Compensation for customers or losses from downtime would erase the advantage sooner.

What can you delegate today?

Start with work that comes up often and is easy to check. If reviewing a result takes as long as doing the work yourself, a more expensive model may just add another bill.

Support

Automate a routine order status check when the system can see the order. Pass disputes, unusual returns and conflicting records to a person with the relevant history attached.

Documents and finance

Extract fields and prepare a reconciliation. Check totals and duplicates with ordinary rules. Supplier approval and payment of a disputed invoice remain human decisions.

Software development

Let an agent draft tests, documentation or a patch. Keep responsibility for release, access changes and hard-to-reverse decisions with people. Count the time to a working release, including review.

Operations

Use software to flag unusual readings and show the machine’s history. Follow the established procedure for deciding whether to stop the line. Spotting common faults is not enough reason to let the system handle rare failures on its own.

What to check before changing headcount

  1. Pick a measurable process. Start with time spent on standard order-status enquiries, not an instruction to replace two operators. When a database field and a rule can do the job, compare against conventional automation too.
  2. See how the team works today. What does resolving a case cost, how long does the customer wait, and how often do they come back? Look separately at difficult cases and busy days.
  3. Try it on real tickets before cutting staff. Start with suggestions to the agent. Compare similar cases, include reviews and corrections, and decide up front what kind of mistake would stop the trial.
  4. Decide what the bot can do on its own. Report an order status, yes. Send a disputed refund to a person. Give that person time to check it and the authority to change the decision.
  5. Decide what you will do with the extra hours. Cut overtime, clear the queue or take on more work. Talk about staffing once you know what the service costs and how well it works on bad days as well as good ones.

Before cutting a job, check both queues: the customers still waiting for a resolution and the colleagues now doing the review and repair work. If the work has simply moved between queues, there is nothing to cut yet.
