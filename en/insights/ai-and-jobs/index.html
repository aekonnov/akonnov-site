Will AI replace workers? The cost of automation

Even team leaders were taking calls. That is how Commonwealth Bank workers described their support desk to the union after a voice bot went live. The bank said call volumes were falling and announced 45 job cuts. In August 2025, it reversed the decision. The case raises questions for both the employer and the market selling it AI: whose work went away, who got paid and who will pick up the rest of the bill?

Anton Konnov · 27 November 2026

Who picks up the phone?

Kathryn Sullivan had worked at Commonwealth Bank for 25 years. She helped write scripts that staff used to answer customers over messaging. In July 2025, she and 44 colleagues were told their jobs would be cut. “I wasn’t even thinking about AI until my job got replaced by a Chatbot,” she told the union.

In August the bank reversed its decision. Staff could stay in their roles or leave with a payment. A company can withdraw a notice. It cannot give people back the weeks they spent wondering how they would pay their bills.

I would not take that as an argument against voice bots. Before changing the rota, I would check how many problems the bot actually solves and how much work it leaves for people.

What work actually went away?

“Where is my order?” has an answer you can look up. Show the customer the status and there is no need for a member of staff to open the order. You may not need a language model either. “I’ve been charged twice” starts a different conversation. Someone has to reconcile the payments, work out what happened and issue a refund if one is due. AI can find the records or draft a reply. If a person still sees the case through, they still have that work.

Forget the reply counter for a moment and look at the customer’s history. If they had to come back with the same problem, the first answer was only the beginning. If the query was resolved and nobody had to open the order, some work really did go away. Whether enough of it went away to remove a shift is a question for the rota, particularly at peak times.

You saved time. What happened to the money?

Take a support desk that resolves 2,000 enquiries a month. Without AI, each takes twelve minutes on average, at a staff cost of RUB 1,500 per hour. With AI, checking a reply takes four minutes and 15% of cases need another fifteen minutes. Model usage, operations and support cost RUB 160,000 a month. The example assumes equally good service in both cases; it is not a quote or a case study.

In the model, handling costs fall by RUB 127,500. In the bank account it is the other way round: keep payroll at RUB 600,000 and there is another RUB 160,000 to pay. The business has bought back roughly 192 hours a month. They could clear a queue, avoid overtime or make room for new orders. Unused, they do not pay the technology bill.

What if more cases need a person than expected? At these rates, the modelled handling costs are the same once 640 enquiries out of 2,000 need extra handling: 32%. At 35%, the AI calculation comes to RUB 622,500. Costly mistakes, compensation or downtime would erase the advantage sooner. And the first good month does not pay for installation: a one-off setup cost of RUB 450,000 takes about 3.5 months to recover only if the business actually gains RUB 127,500 each month after running costs.

Why the AI bill is rarely just the subscription

The table puts technology at RUB 160,000 a month. In practice you first have to work that number out. Reading an email is different from resolving a ticket: the latter might need a document search, a call to an accounts system, several model responses and a retry. On usage-based plans those steps add up. The supplier’s bill does not show the minutes a member of staff spent correcting a wrong answer.

Moving too fast adds a different cost. The company pays severance; if the shift cannot cope, it pays to recruit and train again. The technology contract is already running by then. A business can find itself paying for AI, for people to leave and for people to come back to the same work.

Where companies cut jobs and where they hired again

In 2026, Gartner surveyed 275 service and support leaders. Twenty-one per cent reported cutting headcount because of AI. Seven per cent of all respondents said they had hired again after previous reductions and 11% planned to do so within 18 months. On a separate question, 4% reported getting the return they expected. The survey does not tell us how much money the companies that cut staff saved.

Among the respondents that cut headcount because of AI, the average reduction over the preceding 18 months was 16.7%. Hiring freezes were reported by 45% of all respondents and transfers to other units by 39%. A company could do both.

A separate Gartner study on spending found that 3% of organisations surpassed their savings targets through productivity gains. Thirty-eight per cent spent more on AI than planned, and 85% intended to increase spending the following year. Save two hundred thousand instead of a promised million and you have missed the target, but you did save money.

Where an assistant did help

Other teams saw a different result. A study of 5,172 agents at one company found that an assistant increased resolved enquiries per hour by 15% on average, with the biggest gains for less experienced workers. That was a measured gain for people still serving customers. It was not a test of what would happen without them.

In Russia, the HSE institute’s analysis of Rosstat’s survey found that 5.2% of the large and midsize organisations surveyed used AI in 2025. Among users, 62.7% reported better business processes, 24.1% higher labour productivity and 9.1% a smaller workforce. The survey covered several types of AI, not just generative models, and excluded small businesses.

Hiring can change without layoffs

A manager keeps one analyst and never advertises for a second. No one receives a notice, but there is one less chance for someone trying to enter the profession. The November update from Stanford Digital Economy Lab found that employment among 22-to-25-year-olds in the two most AI-exposed groups of occupations in its US ADP sample was down nearly 4% year-on-year in September 2026. Those data cannot establish why. The practical question for managers is what work will teach beginners to spot mistakes if all the simple tasks are handed to software.

Meta counted more changes. Users saw a different number.

In support it is easy to confuse a reply with a resolution. In software it is easy to confuse a code change with a working feature. Reuters reviewed Meta’s internal records: changes to internal systems rose 220% year-on-year, while changes delivering a new or improved feature to users rose 36%. Major technical and security incidents rose 40%, and staff spent 70% more time dealing with them. These are four different measures. Counting new changes misses the work of the people who have to fix things afterwards.

Under Project OT, Meta considered scenarios in which some teams would shrink by up to 60%. It cut about 10% of its company-wide workforce in May 2026 and called off planning for the second round before the first. Reuters did not establish why management changed course. The practical question remains: when code gets written faster, do review, release and incident response get cheaper too?

In manufacturing, the boundary is clearer. Ford’s vehicle hardware engineering chief Charles Poon describes AI helping engineers spot signs of quality problems. That is different from asking software to decide whether a vehicle can be released. The value of a warning is whether it helps someone make the right call in time.

Who gets paid while the buyer is still looking for savings?

The same compute bill is an expense for the buyer and revenue for the seller. The supplier can report the sale. The buyer still has to find out how much work it bought.

For the quarter ended October 25, 2026, NVIDIA reported revenue of $115.8 billion and net income of $65.3 billion. Its Data Center business accounted for $107.6 billion in revenue. Compute is already an enormous market. But if a support desk pays for it and still needs the same shifts, its supplier’s profit is not a saving for the desk.

When the supplier is also the buyer

Take one contract. In 2025 CoreWeave disclosed an agreement with NVIDIA with an initial value of $6.3 billion. Subject to its terms, NVIDIA agreed to buy cloud capacity CoreWeave did not sell to its other customers through April 2032. In January 2026 NVIDIA separately invested $2 billion in CoreWeave shares. CoreWeave runs on NVIDIA infrastructure. One participant supplies the platform, invests in the cloud operator and stands ready to buy capacity if third-party demand falls short.

That can make it easier to build a site: the supplier takes on some risk of unused capacity. To understand whether the site pays for itself, ask how much compute people outside those financing arrangements will buy. The IMF examines these funding links; the Bank of England estimated that bond issuance by the biggest cloud companies would exceed $305 billion in 2026. That is expected borrowing by that group, not a loss estimate or debt on one CoreWeave contract.

What you might pay for without using AI

Power capacity

A data centre needs not only electricity but enough power available at peak times. In the US PJM grid, the independent market monitor estimated a $29.4 billion increase in capacity-market costs tied to existing and projected data-centre demand. This is a market-level figure, not a household bill. The policy dispute is about rates: what the large user pays and what is spread across other customers.

Debt in an investment portfolio

A loan to build a data centre can stay with its lender or be pooled with other loans to back securities. Matthew Scherer and Maya Jenkins describe how those securities can end up with insurers and pension funds. While borrowers pay, it is an income-producing asset. If a site cannot service its debt, some of the risk sits with whoever owns the security. That is a way for risk to travel, not a prediction that pensions will be cut.

Memory for computers and phones

Factories divide output between servers, PCs and mobile devices. In its fourth-quarter 2026 forecast, TrendForce noted that suppliers were prioritising server memory. It projected quarter-on-quarter contract price rises of 10–15% for conventional DRAM and 15–20% for NAND flash. Those are component prices, not the rise in price of each finished notebook. But a manufacturer building a device with no AI features is still buying memory in that market.

These are three different routes: rates, credit risk and component prices. None requires using a chatbot.

How I would test it in my own department

I would start with one queue, say delivery questions and returns, and find out how much time it takes today. Then I would compare like-for-like cases handled by hand, with ordinary rules and with AI. If an order number and a field in the database are enough to answer the customer, I would choose the simplest option.

A mistake on a disputed refund costs someone money, not just another message. I would let AI collect the payment records, keep the decision with a person and see whether the extra help makes the case quicker to resolve. Other work needs its own measure: in software, for instance, count the time to a working release, including review, not the number of changes.

I would look at a typical week and a peak day, not a good demo. How many cases are resolved the first time? How many come back? How much staff time goes into handling the work, reviewing the output and recovering from failure? Add integration, support and corrections to the model bill. If the same shift still has to work and be paid, an AI charge is an extra cost, not a payroll saving.

The staffing question comes after two answers: what work no longer needs a person, and what happened to the money? Until then I would keep people on the rota. The customer does not care what the automation plan said. They need someone to solve the problem.

Further reading and figures

Why surveys and experiments give different answers

In a METR experiment, early-2025 tools made experienced developers take 19% longer on familiar projects; its follow-up could not estimate the effect with the same confidence because of selection issues. In a survey of nearly 6,000 executives across four countries, more than 80% reported no impact on employment or productivity in the preceding three years. In Denmark, the latest survey round covered 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces. Users reported saving 2.8% of work time on average, but matching the surveys to administrative records ruled out average effects greater than 2% on earnings or recorded hours after two years.

What the employment studies actually measure

In a Stanford study using ADP data through June 2026, employment among 22-to-25-year-olds in more exposed occupations was 19% below where it would have been had they kept pace with less exposed peers. The gap arose mainly through lower hiring rather than layoffs. In the November update, employment in the sample was down 2.2% year-on-year for ages 22–34 and up 0.4% for ages 35 and over. A study across 41 countries found a smaller share of junior staff in foreign affiliates of firms adopting AI, mainly because senior employment rose, not necessarily because the number of junior workers fell.

Challenger recorded 121,401 announced US job cuts citing AI from January to October 2026. That is a set of announcements, not a count of actual layoffs. The ILO evaluated tasks: one in four workers globally were in occupations with some potential generative AI exposure and 3.3% of employment was in the highest group. An estimate of affected tasks cannot tell us how many people will lose their jobs.
