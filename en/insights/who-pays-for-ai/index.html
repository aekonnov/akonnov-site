Who pays for AI? From subscriptions to your own server

A $20 AI subscription can see you through a month of emails. Working on an app can exhaust the allowance in an evening. Your request was a line long, but the assistant is already back in the same code for another pass. The subscription price tells you little about how much work that line sets in motion. Meanwhile, cloud companies are spending hundreds of billions on data centres. Who pays for all this, and when is a business better off running its own server?

Where the money goes while AI builds an app

“Add email sign-in.” The assistant edits the code and runs a test. The test fails, so the assistant sends the updated code and the error message to the model. If the next fix does not work either, it tries again. You wrote one line; the model has already worked through the same code several times.

A developer called wartzar-bee took the logs from 66 of his Claude Code sessions and priced them at API rates. The total was about $2,651. Just 15% was for the model’s output. Reprocessing code, messages and command results it had already seen accounted for 60%. In a typical session, reprocessing accounted for about a quarter of the cost. A handful of long sessions drove up the total.

A long job does not make a $20 ChatGPT Plus subscription more expensive. You can, however, hit the limit before finishing the job. When an assistant uses the API, the provider bills for tokens, the small pieces of text the model reads and writes. Tools such as search may cost extra. Providers sometimes charge less for text they have cached. Even small charges add up if the assistant sends the same material dozens of times.

A job that once needed an expensive model can cost very little a couple of years later. The Stanford AI Index estimated that a million tokens at GPT‑3.5’s score on the MMLU general knowledge test went from $20 in November 2022 to $0.07 in October 2024. That is one test, not every job. Cheaper answers have not stopped investors spending on what models might do next. In March 2026, OpenAI announced $122 billion in funding commitments.

What data centre builders are counting on

Racks, grid connections and cooling cost money before a new site earns any revenue. In September, Wharton estimated capital spending by Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle at $755 billion in 2026 and more than $1 trillion in 2027. That is an investment forecast for five companies, not a bill for model training. When a tenant pays for compute, the operator books revenue. Adding the tenant’s payment to the operator’s construction bill would not give you a better estimate of what it cost to build the site.

Economists Jessica and Jonathan Wachter asked how much more productive the AI sector would have to become to explain spending on that scale. An early version put AI-sector productivity at 2.7 times its previous level. The October revision of the NBER working paper put it at roughly three times. The economy-wide result is different: depending on whether further productivity jumps occur, GDP in 2030 could be 7% to 53% above the baseline across the model’s scenarios.

A data centre does not need to wait for artificial general intelligence to pay off. It can make money from customers regularly buying compute to do jobs such as processing batches of documents. But even an excellent model cannot pay the interest on a loan if the servers sit idle.

Why cloud contracts do not tell you the subscription price

Suppose an AI service owes $700 billion for cloud compute and has 50 million paying subscribers. Spread the bill over five years and charge it all to them, and you get $233 per person per month. Double the subscriber base and let other customers cover half the bill, and you get $58. The cost of the contracts has not changed. Only who pays has changed.

Real contracts have different schedules. Microsoft disclosed that in October 2025 OpenAI agreed to buy an additional $250 billion in Azure services. In February 2026, Amazon and OpenAI announced a $100 billion expansion of their agreement over eight years. OpenAI also brings in revenue from businesses, developers and advertising. The published contract values do not tell us how much of the cost the company will try to recover from subscribers.

The sticker price can stay the same while the work gets more expensive. If the provider cuts the allowance, the team can finish fewer long jobs for the same money. Jobs that no longer fit have to wait or go through a separately billed service. One way costs time; the other puts compute on a new bill. That is one reason companies consider running part of the work in-house.

You can download the model. You still have to buy the memory.

When a model runs on your own server, your documents can stay inside the company and no cloud provider bills you per request. You still pay for the machine, power, setup and repairs. A model can fit in memory and answer one person quickly, then slow down when several people use it at once.

The memory in a server accelerator is not the same as the memory in a laptop, but chipmakers use some of the same manufacturing capacity for both. In October 2025, Samsung and OpenAI announced plans to work together. OpenAI estimated that it could eventually need up to 900,000 silicon wafers a month. These are the discs on which memory chips are made. The figure describes projected demand, not actual shipments. In its October outlook, SK hynix said the shortage could last through 2030.

The shortage is already being felt outside the server market. Counterpoint estimates that manufacturers shipped 19% fewer smartphones in Q3 2026 than a year earlier. It lists tight memory supplies and higher component prices among the reasons.

Who is buying that memory is changing. On 25 November, TrendForce reported that some Chinese cloud companies had cut their planned 2027 purchases of server memory outside long-term agreements. North American buyers took up the supply that became available. TrendForce still expects a shortage in 2027, but forecasts slower increases in contract prices.

For a company considering its own server, the price of memory raises another question: how much will the work actually need? A model that sorts emails or looks things up in internal documents need not be as large as one for complex software development. If a smaller model handles everyday work and the occasional difficult job can safely go to the cloud, the company buys memory for its regular workload, not for the hardest case.

Who makes money before the first app sale?

Suppliers earn revenue when they sell components. Their customers still have to fill the servers with work and earn back the money they spent. In Q3 2026, TSMC reported $46.72 billion in revenue and a 66.4% gross margin. SK hynix reported an 80% operating margin. These figures do not measure the same thing: gross margin deducts the cost of sales, and operating margin also deducts operating expenses.

If you release an app, you pay for models and testing before your first sale. Then you have to find a buyer. When someone pays, the store may take a cut: Apple’s Small Business Program charges participants 15% on eligible sales. A $10 purchase leaves $8.50 before the cost of advertising, running the model and serving users who never buy. If the customer leaves after a month, you do not get back what you spent to attract them. An app that is easier to build is not necessarily easier to sell.

Will demand catch up with construction?

If builders put up more capacity than customers need

An operator with idle servers can cut the rental price. But if providers start to close, their customers may have to accept terms from the survivors or pay to move. In the early 2000s the US International Trade Commission described how operators laid more fibre than demand could support. Some companies went bankrupt; the lines stayed. A data centre's grid connection can likewise outlast its owner, though the accelerators inside it age much faster than fibre cables.

A discount helps the tenant, but the operator still has debts to pay. On 30 September 2026, CoreWeave owed $47.5 billion. Its quarterly filing showed nearly $11 billion of principal due in 2027, almost a quarter of the total. A drop in revenue can hurt shareholders. If the company can no longer make its payments, lenders can lose money too.

In its November report, the Federal Reserve counted more than $680 billion of debt raised for advanced computing infrastructure in the first ten months of 2026. Investment funds, pension funds and insurers are among the holders of debt issued by major cloud and accelerator companies. If that debt is not repaid, losses can reach people who never built or rented a data centre.

If customers use the capacity

Cheaper requests need not mean a smaller bill. Halve the price of a request and send three times as many, and the bill goes up by half. A customer might be willing to pay if it means getting documents processed today instead of tomorrow.

Who pays for expanding the grid?

Paying for electricity is one thing. Paying for a new substation and the lines that connect a data centre is another. In the IEA’s base case, electricity use by data centres worldwide rises from 415 TWh in 2024 to about 950 TWh in 2030. In March 2026, Gallup asked US adults whether they supported an AI data centre in their area; 71% were opposed. The poll did not measure utility rates. How much of a new line the developer pays for, and how much other customers pay through their bills, depends on the terms of the connection.

Cloud or your own server? An example from purchasing

A purchasing team is comparing quotes. One supplier prices by the pack, another by the item, and a third puts the delivery date only in an email. The company imports quotes that follow its template with ordinary software. For the others, a model puts the description, unit price and delivery date on one line. A buyer checks the line and adds it to the comparison sheet. The buyer still decides which supplier gets the order.

In this worked example, both systems produce lines of the same quality and take staff the same time to check. The cloud costs $300 a month to run, plus $0.18 per approved line. An $18,000 server works out to $500 a month over 36 months, with another $700 a month for maintenance, electricity and spare capacity. With its own server, the company still sends difficult quotes to the cloud. Across the whole workload that adds $0.03 per line to the cost of the own-server option.

The fixed costs are $1,200 a month for the server and $300 for the cloud. Processing a line locally saves $0.15 in this example. At 6,000 approved lines, both options cost $1,380 a month. That could be 300 quotes of 20 lines each that could not be imported in the usual way. If the cloud’s $0.18 charge doubles and the server costs stay the same, the two options break even around 2,730 lines. If the cloud charge falls by half instead, they break even at 15,000.

The result can change without either tariff moving. Before a major purchase, dozens of quotes might come in on one day instead of being spread across a month. If the server falls behind or staff have to correct more lines by hand, savings on the cloud bill can go straight into rush processing and checks. A low price per line is not much help if the purchasing team spends longer correcting the work.

What a business keeps if its AI provider changes the terms

The purchasing team has an alternative if it owns the original quotes, the rules for comparing prices and examples of correctly processed lines. It can give another model the same batch and see where the answers differ. If the rules exist only inside the old service, changing provider means rebuilding them.

A fivefold rise in token prices does not have to mean a fivefold increase in the cost of the whole job. If $10 out of every $100 goes to tokens, the same workload costs $140, not $500, with other costs unchanged. That leaves a more useful question: can the business absorb the extra $40, or should it move some jobs to a cheaper model?

App sales create a similar dependency. If every customer comes through one store, its rules affect more than the commission. They also affect how a developer reaches people about the next release. A website or an opt-in mailing list gives customers a way to hear from the developer even if the app changes platforms.
