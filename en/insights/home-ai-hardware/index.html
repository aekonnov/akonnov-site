How to read the estimate

For a single memory pool, add weights, a 16-bit cache estimate for full-attention layers and a reserve for other buffers. Actual usage depends on the engine. DeepSeek shows a lower bound without verified cache. Flash‑Next is separate because weights span system RAM and GPU. The last 10% of capacity is flagged as tight. Model profile source · Weight files.

For a discrete card, compare against VRAM. RAM offload can help to load weights but is not equivalent to GPU residency. Apple, AMD and GB10 share memory with the OS and other workloads. For MoE, count all resident weights, not just active parameters. This calculator does not predict speed: measure time to first token, decoding and load separately.

Hardware and its trade-offs

Once weights fit, compare memory bandwidth and engine support. The 128 GB AMD AI Halo is rated at 256 GB/s, the GB10 at 273 GB/s and the RTX PRO 6000 at 1,792 GB/s. These are hardware specs, not inference benchmarks: a larger pool may fit a larger model, while a smaller card can be faster on a smaller model.

Larger models and server economics

A first computer for local AI

Local AI does not have to start with a GPU purchase. On the machine you already own, try a small Q4 model on three real tasks: summarising a document, asking a code question and carrying on a longer conversation. First find out whether the answers are useful. A faster GPU will not fix the same model's mistakes on a short prompt.

An entry-level PC with 8–16 GB of VRAM suits small models, short prompts and first experiments with image generation. The two ends of that range are not interchangeable. Check the GPU's actual memory: a listing that says “32 GB memory” often means system RAM. Leave SSD space for several versions of the weights rather than filling it with a single download.

If you already have a 16 GB card, you can try a larger model too. In the RTX 5060 Ti test, Flash‑Next generated 62.7 tok/s after a 16K prompt. The budgeting detail: that PC had 128 GB of system RAM. If you are buying from scratch, compare the cost of complete systems, not just GPUs.

When short prompts work but a long document does not fit, try a shorter context, chunking the document or retrieving the relevant passages. If every request really needs the whole document, saving on memory stops making sense. The calculator helps you see that boundary before buying.

GeForce: 24 or 32 GB for everyday work

A used RTX 3090 is attractive primarily for its 24 GB of VRAM at a lower system budget. It makes sense for mid-size text models if the complete build still works out after a suitable power supply. Inspect memory temperatures under sustained load, cooling condition and the ability to test before purchase. Leaving those costs out makes the card look cheaper than it is.

The RTX 5090 has 32 GB: eight extra gigabytes for cache, a less compressed quant or another process, not just more speed. In the Qwen3.8‑27B calculator example, a 32K context needs about 25 GB and 262K about 40 GB. The 5090 addresses the first constraint but not the second. When the long window is essential, compare it with higher-capacity systems as well as the 3090.

The expandable tests show another option: Flash‑Next ran on one 3090 with 64 GB of RAM and CPU expert computation, and on a 5090 with a heavily compressed IQ2_XS checkpoint and a specialised engine. Both are real setups; neither is the same as running NVFP4 entirely on one card. Compare answer quality as well as speed: quantisation and placement matter as much as the GPU label here.

AMD Ryzen AI Max: capacity in a small enclosure

Consider the 128 GB Ryzen AI Max+ 395 and 192 GB PRO 495 when a model does not fit in 24–32 GB of VRAM but a large workstation is unnecessary. CPU and GPU share a pool; adding the two memory figures in a listing double-counts it. Check what your engine can actually allocate with your OS and settings.

On 128 GB Strix Halo, Gufo measured 28.2 tok/s after a 32K prompt without speculative drafting, using Flash‑Next UD‑Q4_K_XL. That is evidence for running a large model on a compact system, not a promise for every AMD mini PC or engine. The extra 64 GB on a 192 GB build is room for weights and cache, not an automatic speed gain.

On a 192 GB Minisforum with the PRO 495, a different engine reached 69.4 tok/s on prose and 89.9 on code. Four concurrent requests produced 115 tok/s combined, about 29 per request on average. The single-stream rate matters for a personal chat; the concurrent rate matters for a small team. The difference from Gufo is not a measure of the CPU upgrade: the engine and test conditions also changed.

When comparing mini PCs from different vendors, look beyond the processor: actual memory, power limits, sustained cooling, SSD and warranty all matter. Before paying for the larger configuration, confirm its extra memory is available to the GPU backend you intend to use.

DGX Spark and GB10: when two nodes are worth it

A 128 GB Spark brings CUDA and a large shared pool into a compact box. Its ARM64 CPU means a prebuilt x86 container or extension may not work. NInfer ran Flash‑Next on one Spark with disk-backed PLE, and on a pair over 200 Gb/s with parallel execution. A short single-request test yielded 70 and 109.5 tok/s respectively: the second box helped, but did not double speed.

There are two different reasons to buy a second Spark. Splitting one model needs a compatible engine, a direct interconnect and a memory budget per node. For independent work, it may be simpler to run separate models or queues, with no network exchange for each generated token. Do not buy a second node just to reach 256 GB on paper: identify which job it will actually do.

Mac Studio: what you get beyond 96 GB

Choose Mac Studio around a verified Metal/MLX workflow. The 96 GB version makes sense when weights and cache fit with room to spare. Flash‑Next offers a more specific reference point: an M5 Ultra 256 GB with MLX 6-bit and TensorFold reached 116–132 tok/s after a 32K prompt across two tasks. A separate 262K-window test used about 188 GiB above idle at its peak. That gives a concrete reason to buy 256 GB for this workload rather than simply chasing capacity.

In the 96 GB test, a smaller Flash‑Next build reached 111–153 tok/s on short prompts and 88–116 after 177K, with a 69-second wait for the first token. In the 512 GB test, 6-bit weights generated at 85 tok/s after a million input tokens, but the first token arrived almost seven minutes later. The formats and engines differ. For choosing a Mac, waiting time on documents of your own length matters more than the headline speed.

At the listed prices, going from the $9,499 256 GB configuration to 512 GB from $22,299 adds about $12,800. Justify it with a workload that genuinely needs more than 256 GB: larger weights, multiple models or a bigger cache. Extra memory will not fix slow document ingestion or a CUDA-only plugin. Memory cannot be added later, but buying a large speculative reserve is costly.

Workstations: new RTX PRO or a used server accelerator

An RTX PRO 6000 makes sense when you need 96 GB of fast dedicated memory and familiar CUDA tools, not merely a way to load large weights. The $15–25k estimate buys a workstation, not the card alone. In the lab this card serves text models and heavy video generation, but not simultaneously: the text model releases the GPU first. If both services must run together, specify that before purchasing.

Used cards bring different trade-offs. The lab ran Qwen3.8‑27B Q4 on a 32 GB V100, but that tells you nothing about support for newer kernels or formats. RTX A6000 has 48 GB; A100 comes in 40 and 80 GB versions. Compare complete systems: SXM does not plug into an ordinary PCIe slot, and passively cooled cards need engineered airflow. Budget for memory checks and a sustained load test, not just a successful driver startup.

Chinese devices: distinguish the box from the compute platform

A Chinese mini PC can use the same AMD processor as a more expensive workstation, but compare exact configurations. Ask for the installed memory, power settings, return terms and who services the machine. The right chip does not make up for a slow SSD or a backend allocation limit.

Ascend is a different category: the card, CANN version, supported vLLM profile, model and weight format must work together. A chip name and memory capacity are not enough. Ask for a demonstration of the intended model, one real request and a successful start after a reboot. Compare against a complete CUDA system with adaptation and support included, not against a bare board price.

Cost of ownership: buying versus API

Compare the same workload over the same period. Three-year local cost is purchase, electricity, maintenance and setup time minus resale value. Cloud cost is the actual monthly bill plus anything the subscription excludes. A machine rarely solves the main problem when a heavy model is needed only a couple of days a month. Daily workloads or data that cannot leave the local environment change the trade-off.

An illustrative budget, not a payback forecast: a $4,000 PC drawing an average of 250 W at the wall for eight hours daily, with electricity at $0.20/kWh. Power costs about $438 over three years; with purchase, $4,438, or roughly $123 per month before support and resale. A $60 cloud bill totals $2,160 over the same period; $180 totals $6,480. This only matters if the local model delivers the work at acceptable quality and latency.

A practical compromise is a small local setup for documents, retrieval and recurring work, with rare heavy jobs sent to an external service only where policy allows. Before buying, inspect a month of actual use: short versus long requests, concurrency, and failures due to model quality. Those numbers beat advertised TOPS or a vague claim of support for “several users”.

How to choose and accept a system

Write down the model and weight format, working prompt length and how many people really request it at once. Shortlist two systems by memory and price. On a vendor demo or rented machine, check three things: first-token latency on a long document, answer speed on your task and behaviour under concurrent load. A laptop chat launch and a service acceptance test are different checks.

For chat and small documents, start with the machine you own or an entry PC. For fast mid-size models, compare the 3090 and 5090 at your own context length. If you need more memory, choose between AMD, Spark and Mac based on the supported software. A workstation GPU or cluster makes sense when it removes a measured bottleneck. Prices and models may shift in 2027; these checks survive the next GPU release.
